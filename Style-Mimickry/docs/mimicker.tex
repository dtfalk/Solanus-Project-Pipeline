\begin{abstract}
This document explains the \code{Style-Mimickry} toolkit: a small system that makes a
\emph{frozen} large language model write in my voice and then \emph{measures} whether it
succeeded. Because I have forbidden fine-tuning, none of the style can live in the model's
weights; it must instead be \emph{carried into} the model at request time (retrieved real examples
of my writing) and \emph{measured from outside} (an embedding ``center of mass'' of my prose plus
an interpretable stylometry audit). The toolkit reuses the \code{step\_7} libraries wholesale, so
every model call is cost-logged and the model itself is a swappable configuration variable. Nothing
here trains, downloads a large model, or spends money until explicitly asked.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem, and why ``no fine-tuning'' reshapes it}
% =============================================================================

I want a model to write the way I write. The usual answer is to fine-tune --- to nudge the weights
until the model's default voice \emph{becomes} mine. I have ruled that out. That single constraint
is clarifying rather than merely limiting: if the style cannot live in the weights, then it has to
live somewhere I can \emph{see}, and the whole design becomes inspectable. There is no mysterious
tensor that ``knows'' my voice; there is a folder of my actual sentences and a number that says how
close a candidate is to them.

So I split the word ``voice'' into two jobs:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Carry} the voice into the model at request time, by retrieving real examples of my
        writing and showing them to the model (few-shot prompting).
  \item \textbf{Measure} the voice from the outside, with an embedding centroid of my prose and a
        plain-English stylometry dashboard.
\end{enumerate}

One half makes the model \emph{sound} like me; the other half tells me, honestly, whether it did.
The three Python files map one-to-one onto this: \code{data\_prep.py} builds the materials,
\code{generate.py} carries the voice in, \code{evaluate.py} measures it.

\paragraph{A note on what we deliberately omit.} The research report's strongest recommendation is
a fine-tuned hybrid (synthetic neutral$\to$styled pairs, QLoRA, a reconstruction objective). All of
that is gone, on purpose. What survives is exactly the subset that works on a frozen model:
retrieved-exemplar prompting, a RAG index, a style centroid, a (prompt-proxy) StyleVector nudge, a
gated Horikawa refinement loop, and the interpretable evaluation.

\section{Data preparation: the real product}
% =============================================================================

\code{data\_prep.py} reads my writings --- at present the \code{.tex} explainers in
\code{examples/writing\_examples} --- and does three things.

\paragraph{1) Strip the scaffolding.} My style lives in the sentences, not in
\code{\textbackslash usepackage\{tikz\}}. So before anything else we remove the LaTeX preamble, the
figure/\code{tikzpicture}/\code{lstlisting} environments, inline math, and stray commands, leaving a
prose approximation. This is best-effort and regex-only; it does not need a real LaTeX parser,
because the goal is just to clean the \emph{style signal}, not to render the document.

\paragraph{2) Segment into exemplars.} We cut the prose into passages of roughly $40$--$180$ words.
That band is not arbitrary. The stylometry literature warns that count-based features are essentially
noise below $\sim$100 words and get diluted across too many ideas above $\sim$180, so a
paragraph-sized window is the sweet spot. Each exemplar keeps a pointer back to the file it came
from, so anything I later show the model --- or that the evaluator flags --- can be traced to my
actual writing.

\paragraph{3) Embed (only with \code{--embed}).} The one paid step. We embed every exemplar with the
\code{step\_7} model-agnostic embedder, write them to a partitioned vector store under a
\code{style::model@dim} space, and save the \textbf{style centroid}.

\paragraph{What the centroid is, intuitively.} Imagine every passage I have written as a point in a
high-dimensional space where ``near'' means ``stylistically similar.'' The centroid is the
\emph{center of mass} of that cloud --- the single point that best summarizes ``the average of my
voice.'' Because the embedder returns unit vectors, asking ``how close is this new text to the
centroid?'' is just a cosine,
\[
  \cossim(\mathbf{v}_{\text{text}}, \mathbf{c})
  \;=\; \mathbf{v}_{\text{text}} \cdot \mathbf{c}
  \quad\in [-1, 1],
\]
where bigger means more like me. That one number is the target the refinement loop steers toward and
the anchor the evaluator scores against.

\begin{lstlisting}
python data_prep.py            # FREE: segment + write exemplars.jsonl + profile
python data_prep.py --embed    # PAID: also embed -> RAG index + style_centroid.npy
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Generation in three layers}
% =============================================================================

The layers are ordered cheapest-and-most-reliable first, exactly as the research recommends.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Layer 1 --- retrieved exemplars + few-shot (runs today)}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

Given a prompt, we embed it, pull the $k$ exemplars nearest it from the index, and lay them in front
of a \emph{frozen} model with a simple instruction: \emph{here is how David writes; answer in that
voice; imitate the manner, not the content.} This is the workhorse and the honest baseline. Telling a
model ``write like David'' is weak; showing it the three paragraphs of mine most relevant to
\emph{this exact topic} is strong, because it anchors the model on my subject and my manner at once.

\paragraph{Why ``manner, not content.''} A frozen model reproduces surface voice well but will
cheerfully \emph{confabulate} opinions I never expressed --- this is documented and unavoidable
without retrieval-grounded stance modelling. So the system instruction explicitly forbids inventing
my views, and the evaluation measures \emph{style}, never belief.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Layer 2 --- a StyleVector-style nudge (scaffolded)}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is a lovely result (StyleVector) that a person's style is roughly a single \emph{direction} in
a model's activation space, and that you can steer a frozen model along it with no training. The
catch is that you must reach inside the model, and a closed API will not let you. The version I can
run honestly today is therefore a \emph{described} direction: I write down the axes where my voice
leans --- analogy first, explain the \emph{why}, vary the sentence rhythm --- and inject that
description into the prompt. The genuine activation-space method is left as a clearly marked TODO that
needs a small open model with hidden-state access; the steering equation it would implement is
\[
  \mathbf{h}' \;=\; \mathbf{h} \;+\; \alpha\,\mathbf{s},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{s} \;=\;
  \overline{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{authentic}} - \overline{\mathbf{h}}_{\text{style-agnostic}},
\]
i.e.\ add a scaled style vector $\mathbf{s}$ (the contrast between activations on authentic vs.\
style-agnostic completions) to the residual stream.

% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Layer 3 --- Horikawa-style refinement (scaffolded and gated)}
% -----------------------------------------------------------------------------

This is the ambitious part. Horikawa's ``mind captioning'' evolves a sentence so that its
meaning-vector matches a target \emph{decoded from a brain}. The transfer is almost too clean:
replace ``brain-decoded target'' with ``my style centroid'' and you get a loop that repeatedly
\textbf{masks} a few words, asks a masked language model to \textbf{propose} natural fillers, and
\textbf{keeps} whichever candidate's style embedding is closest to my centroid --- while a length
penalty stops the search from cheating by collapsing to something trivially short.

I implemented the control flow and, importantly, the \emph{fitness function}, which I can compute
right now because it is just cosine-to-centroid times a length penalty:
\[
  \text{fitness}(x)
  \;=\;
  \underbrace{\cossim\!\big(\mathbf{v}_x,\, \mathbf{c}\big)}_{\text{closeness to my voice}}
  \;\times\;
  \underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{\ell}{\ell_0}\Big)^{-\alpha}}_{\text{length penalty } (\alpha \approx 0.1)},
\]
where $\ell$ is the candidate's word count and $\ell_0$ the target length. The one missing piece is
the masked-LM proposer, which needs RoBERTa-large locally --- a heavy download I have not approved
--- so the loop is \code{enabled=False} by default. If you turn it on, it reports exactly what it
would do and changes nothing; it is non-destructive by construction (it only ever returns a
\emph{new} string).

\begin{lstlisting}
python generate.py "Explain entropy to a beginner."        # DRY RUN (no API calls)
python generate.py "Explain entropy to a beginner." --run  # PAID: actually generate
python generate.py "..." --run --steer --refine            # Layer 2 + (gated) Layer 3
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Evaluation: three honest measurements}
% =============================================================================

A model that \emph{feels} like me on one example tells me nothing. The evaluator scores a candidate
three ways, and the three together are far harder to fool than any one alone.

\paragraph{1) Neural cosine (primary).} Cosine of the candidate's embedding to my centroid, in the
spirit of LUAR / StyleDistance. We also report the cosine to the \emph{single nearest} exemplar,
because a bland, generic output can drift toward the bland average and score deceptively well; it is
much harder to also sit close to a real, specific passage of mine.

\paragraph{2) Interpretable stylometry (the dashboard I actually read).} We profile my exemplars on a
small, robust, dependency-free feature set --- function-word rates (pronouns, articles, prepositions:
the famously involuntary, topic-independent tells), sentence-length mean and variance (rhythm and
burstiness), punctuation habits, vocabulary richness, mean word length --- and report, per feature, a
$z$-score: how many standard deviations the candidate sits from my range,
\[
  z_f \;=\; \frac{x_f - \mu_f}{\sigma_f}.
\]
The output reads like a teacher's margin notes: \emph{``sentences too long ($z=+1.2$), too few
first-person pronouns ($z=-2.4$).''} Embeddings can tell you \emph{that} something is off; only this
tells you \emph{what}, and it is the tripwire against a candidate that games the embedding.

\paragraph{3) Authorship-attribution discriminator (the adversarial judge).} The strongest honest
test of a clone is whether a classifier can separate it from the real thing. The proper version
trains a small model on my writing versus the clone's and reports how easily it separates them ---
closer to a coin flip is better. That needs scikit-learn, which is not yet in the environment, so for
now there is a transparent nearest-centroid fallback that returns a ``clone-probability,'' and the
trained classifier is a clearly marked TODO.

\begin{lstlisting}
python evaluate.py                       # FREE: sanity-check on a real exemplar
python evaluate.py "some generated text" # FREE: stylometry + discriminator
python evaluate.py "..." --neural        # PAID: also embed for the cosine metric
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Why this is honest about its limits}
% =============================================================================

Two failure modes are worth saying out loud, because both are documented in the research.

\paragraph{Embedding metrics can be gamed.} You can land near the centroid while sounding nothing
like me. That is precisely why the stylometry dashboard exists as a second, independent check.

\paragraph{Interpretable features can also be gamed.} You can hit my function-word rates while
butchering the syntax. That is why the embedding cosine and (eventually) the discriminator sit beside
the dashboard. No single number is trustworthy; the three together are much harder to fool, and a
human who knows my writing remains the final judge.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Cost and safety.} Every model call routes through \code{lib.costlog}, so it lands in
\code{step\_7/costs/usage.csv} like any archival run. The default runs are \emph{free}:
\code{data\_prep.py} without \code{--embed}, \code{generate.py} without \code{--run}, and
\code{evaluate.py} without \code{--neural} make zero API calls. Everything is written under
\code{Style-Mimickry/data/}; my source writings are only ever read. No training, no fine-tuning, and
no large downloads happen anywhere.

% =============================================================================
\section{Caveats}
% =============================================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Confabulated opinions are the real danger.} The model can invent stances I never
        held. The guardrail is the ``manner, not content'' instruction plus the fact that the
        evaluation only scores style. Faithful \emph{opinions} would be a different, harder system.
  \item \textbf{Short text is unreliable.} Count-based features wobble on short inputs; judge on
        paragraph-sized outputs and aggregate over several.
  \item \textbf{The corpus is small for now.} Three explainers is enough to prototype, not to capture
        the full range of my voice; the fix is to point \code{CORPUS\_DIRS} at more of my writing.
  \item \textbf{The neuroscience analogy has a seam.} Horikawa optimizes toward a \emph{measured}
        neural target; here the target is a corpus-derived embedding, itself a lossy proxy for ``my
        voice.'' Promising, but a final polish, not the foundation.
\end{itemize}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Summary.} The toolkit takes one idea seriously --- if I cannot put my voice in the
weights, I will carry it in the prompt and measure it from the outside --- and follows it all the way
down: real exemplars retrieved per request, a centroid that is the center of mass of my prose, and an
evaluation that reports both \emph{how} David-like and \emph{which dimensions} are off. The advanced
layers (StyleVector steering, Horikawa refinement) are scaffolded so the research path is visible,
but gated so nothing heavy or paid runs until I say so.
